Foundation

The Discovery Gap

Most founders skip real customer research. They build on assumptions — guesses dressed up as insights. The gap between what you think customers want and what they actually need is where startups go to die. This week, we close that gap with data from real conversations.

The Interview-to-Insight Framework

🎧

Listen, Don't Pitch

A validation interview is not a sales conversation. Your job is to understand, not to convince. The moment you start explaining your solution, you stop learning.

Rule: If you talked more than 30% of the time, it wasn't an interview.

📊

Pattern Recognition

One interview tells you a story. Five interviews reveal a pattern. Ten interviews give you confidence. You need volume to separate individual quirks from market truths.

Threshold: 5-10 interviews before drawing any conclusions.

🎯

Signal vs Noise

People tell you what they think you want to hear. What they do matters more than what they say. Look for actions: money spent, time invested, workarounds built.

Test: Have they spent money or time trying to solve this already?

Discovery in Action: Uganda Case Studies

Boda-Boda Delivery Networks

Assumption

Riders' main problem is finding customers. Build an app to connect riders with delivery requests.

What Interviews Revealed

After speaking with 8 boda-boda riders across Kampala, a founder discovered that finding customers wasn't the core issue at all. Riders had plenty of work. Their real struggle was managing cash flow between jobs — fuel costs, phone credit, and daily savings targets created a constant liquidity crunch.

The Pivot

Instead of a delivery marketplace, the founder explored micro-lending and daily savings tools tailored to the boda-boda economy.

Key Insight

The real problem wasn't demand — it was cash flow. Without interviews, this founder would have built the wrong product entirely.

🏫

School Fees Collection

Assumption

Parents struggle to pay school fees. Build a mobile money payment tool to make fees easier to pay.

What Interviews Revealed

A fintech founder interviewed 10 parents across Wakiso and Mukono districts. Payment itself wasn't the frustration. Parents already used mobile money. The real pain was tracking multiple children across different schools — different fee structures, different deadlines, different bank accounts. They were losing track and paying late fees unnecessarily.

The Pivot

The product shifted from a payment tool to a fee tracking and reminder system that aggregated all children's school obligations in one dashboard.

Key Insight

The problem wasn't paying — it was organizing. Interviews separated the obvious symptom from the actual pain point.

🏪

Market Vendor Inventory

Assumption

Market vendors need a digital inventory system to track their stock. Build an app that lets them scan and manage products.

What Interviews Revealed

After 7 conversations with vendors in Owino Market and Nakasero, the founder realized vendors already knew their stock — they could recite it from memory. What they desperately needed was access to credit to restock when demand spiked. The inventory "problem" was a solution looking for a customer.

The Pivot

The founder shifted to a micro-credit model using vendor transaction history (via mobile money) as a proxy for creditworthiness.

Key Insight

Vendors didn't need stock tracking — they needed credit access. The founder's assumption about the problem was completely wrong.

Framework

Building Your Customer Persona

You've talked to 5-10 people. Now it's time to stop thinking about individuals and start building a composite — a single, vivid character who represents your target customer. A good persona isn't a spreadsheet row. It's a person you can name, describe, and empathise with.

Three Dimensions · Select a Tab
👤

Demographics & Context

The basic facts that define who your customer is and where they operate.

Core Demographics

Age range, gender, location, income level, occupation, education, and family structure. Be specific — "25-35 year old mothers in Kampala" is better than "women in Uganda."

Uganda-Specific Context

Urban vs peri-urban vs rural. Formal economy vs informal economy. Mobile money usage level (MTN MoMo, Airtel Money). Language preferences. Access to smartphone vs feature phone. These factors shape what solutions are even possible.

Economic Reality

Monthly income range, income stability (salaried vs daily earnings), savings behaviour, and existing financial obligations. Understanding economic context prevents you from pricing yourself out of your own market.

💔

Pain Points & Motivations

The emotional core of your persona — what drives them and what holds them back.

What Keeps Them Up at Night

The anxieties, frustrations, and fears that relate to the problem you're solving. Use their actual words from interviews. "I never know if I'll have enough for school fees next term" is more powerful than "financial uncertainty."

What They've Already Tried

Previous attempts to solve the problem — other products, workarounds, manual processes. What worked partially? What failed? Why did they stop using past solutions? This tells you what your product must beat.

What "Solved" Looks Like

Map the emotional journey from frustration to resolution. What does your customer's life look like when this problem no longer exists? Paint that picture — it becomes your product's promise.

📱

Behavior & Channels

How your customer moves through the world — and where you can reach them.

Where They Spend Time

WhatsApp groups, Facebook communities, market days, church or mosque gatherings, trading centres, SACCOs, village savings groups. Understanding their daily rhythms tells you when and where to show up.

How They Find Solutions

Word of mouth from neighbours? Radio advertisements? Posters at trading centres? Agent networks? WhatsApp forwards? The channel your customer trusts is the channel you must use.

Technology Comfort

Do they use apps or just USSD? Do they type or prefer voice notes? Are they comfortable with digital payments or do they prefer cash? Your product's interface must match their comfort level, not yours.

The Persona Pyramid

The strongest personas are built from the bottom up. Start with needs, then understand behaviors, then layer on demographics. Most founders do this backwards — they start with who, but never get to why.

Framework

TAM, SAM, SOM — In Plain Language

Market sizing is not about impressing investors with big numbers. It's about understanding whether the market you're entering is large enough to sustain a business — and small enough that you can actually reach it.

Common Market Sizing Mistakes

❌
"There are 45 million Ugandans, so our market is huge." Population is not a market. Most of those people will never hear of you, need you, or pay you.
❌
Googling "market size" and using someone else's number. Reports from consulting firms describe global markets, not your reality on the ground in Kampala.
❌
Top-down only: starting with GDP and dividing. This approach gives you a number that sounds impressive but has no connection to your actual business model.
❌
Ignoring willingness and ability to pay. A problem can be real and widespread — but if people can't or won't pay to solve it, it's not a market.
❌
Counting everyone who has the problem, not those who'd pay to solve it. The gap between "has the problem" and "would pay for a solution" is enormous.

How to Size It Right

✅
Start bottom-up: how many customers can you actually reach? Count the people you can physically or digitally access through your planned distribution channels.
✅
Use your interview data to estimate conversion. Of the people you talked to, how many showed genuine willingness to pay? That ratio is your conversion estimate.
✅
TAM = total addressable, SAM = serviceable, SOM = obtainable Year 1. Three concentric circles, each one smaller and more honest than the last.
✅
Factor in pricing, distribution channels, and payment methods. A customer who would pay UGX 5,000/month via mobile money is more valuable than one who "might" pay UGX 50,000 in cash.
✅
Be honest: a small, real market beats a large, imaginary one. Investors and partners respect founders who know their numbers. Honesty builds credibility.

Case Study: Eaxit.co

An online marketplace selling African goods to the diaspora in America. Watch how the market narrows from ambition to reality.

Notice how the market narrows at each level. The TAM is everyone who could buy. The SAM is everyone you can reach. The SOM is who you can realistically sell to in Year 1. Investors want to see that you understand the difference.

A Note on Scope — and Honesty

Your TAM could technically be all of Africa — or even the global market for your category. But as an early-stage founder in Uganda, it's more useful to scope your TAM to the country you're operating in. You can always expand later. Right now, you need focus.

More importantly, there's a concept most market sizing frameworks skip: your Initial Target Market (ITM). This is even smaller than your SOM. It's the specific group of customers you'll go after first — your beachhead. The neighbourhood, the WhatsApp group, the market stall row. The people you can physically reach in Week 1.

The Capture Rate Reality Check

Just because your SOM is 5,000 people doesn't mean you'll win all 5,000. You won't. A realistic Year 1 capture rate for a new product might be 2–10% of your SOM. If you're projecting 50% market capture in Year 1, you're not being honest — you're writing fiction. Investors know the difference. The interactive tool below helps you model this honestly.

Action

Your Homework This Week

Refine Your Persona & Size Your Market

This week is about turning raw interview data into structured tools you'll use for the rest of the programme. Your persona and market sizing worksheet will inform every decision from here on — your go-to-market strategy, your pricing, your MVP scope.

01

Review Your Interview Notes

Look for patterns across all 5-10 conversations. What phrases keep coming up? What frustrations were mentioned by 3 or more people? Highlight recurring themes.

02

Build Your Persona

Use the framework above. Give them a name. Make them real. Include demographics, pain points, motivations, and behaviors. One page, not ten.

03

Map the Customer Journey

From problem awareness to seeking a solution to paying for it. Where does your customer first feel the pain? What do they try? Where do they give up? Where can you intervene?

04

Calculate Your TAM

How many people in Uganda (or your target geography) experience this problem? Use census data, industry reports, or extrapolate from your interviews.

05

Calculate Your SAM

Of those, how many can you actually reach through your planned channels? If you're starting in Kampala, don't count rural Uganda. Be honest about your distribution.

06

Calculate Your SOM

Realistically, how many can you convert in Year 1? Use your interview conversion signals. If 6 out of 10 people said they'd pay, apply that ratio conservatively.

07

Define Your ITM (Initial Target Market)

Who are the very first customers you'll go after? This is your beachhead — the specific neighbourhood, WhatsApp group, or market row. If you can't name actual people or places, it's too vague.

08

Set a Realistic Capture Rate

What percentage of your SOM will you actually capture in Year 1? Most startups land between 2–8%. Use the interactive tool above to model your revenue at different capture rates. If your business only works at 30% capture, rethink your pricing or market.

09

Estimate Unit Economics

What will you charge? What does it cost to serve one customer? Even rough numbers matter — they tell you whether this business can sustain itself.
